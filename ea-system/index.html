The EA Operating System

The toolkit I hand a client in week one. Most assistants arrive and wait to be told what to do. This is the alternative: six documents that get filled in during the first fortnight and then run the relationship, so the answer to "what do I do about this?" is written down rather than living in my head.

Why that matters to you. If I disappeared tomorrow, someone else could pick this up and keep your week running. You are not buying a person who knows how things work. You are buying a system that happens to be operated by me.

  1. Onboarding questionnaire what I need before touching anything
  2. Escalation matrix what I handle, draft, or never touch
  3. Inbox rules and templates how email gets triaged
  4. Weekly check-in five minutes of your attention, once a week
  5. Meetings agenda before, actions after
  6. Monthly report what happened, what it cost

Built by Shizzel Brown, executive assistant and content strategist, Accra

Document 01

Onboarding questionnaire

Thirty minutes, once, in week one. It saves the fortnight of small interruptions that usually follows a handover. Thirty-three questions across seven areas: how you want to work, your calendar, your inbox, your people, your tools, your boundaries, and what done looks like.

The questions that do the most work are the uncomfortable ones:

  • What is the largest amount I can spend without asking?
  • What decisions would you be annoyed to find I had made alone?
  • What decisions would you be annoyed to be asked about?
  • What would make you say this was not working?

Those four answers become the escalation matrix, which is the document everything else depends on.

Document 02

Escalation matrix

The most important document in the set. Everything else is admin.

An assistant who asks about everything is a second job. An assistant who asks about nothing is a risk. This is where we agree exactly where that line sits, in writing, so neither of us is guessing.

LaneWhat it meansYou find out
HandleI do it, you never hear about itIn the weekly, if at all
DraftI prepare it, you approve before it goesSame day, batched
EscalateI stop and bring it to youImmediately

The rule for everything not on the list

No matrix anticipates everything. So when something new arrives, I apply one test: is this reversible, and is it cheap to be wrong?

ReversibleCost of being wrongWhat I do
YesLowHandle it, mention it in the weekly
YesHighDraft it and check
NoLowDraft it and check
NoHighEscalate, always

Rescheduling an internal call is reversible and cheap. Sending a quote is neither. That single test means you rarely have to add rows for things nobody anticipated.

How I flag things

I do not drip-feed. Things that can wait get batched, so you are interrupted once rather than eleven times.

UrgencyHow I tell youWhat I expect
NowDirect message, marked urgentInterrupt whatever you are doing
TodayOne batched message, onceAn answer before end of day
This weekWeekly check-inAnswered Monday
NotedWeekly summary lineNo response needed

When I get it wrong

I tell you the same day: what happened, what I have already done about it, and what I am changing so it does not repeat. Then the change goes into this document. The failure mode I am guarding against is an assistant who hides a mistake for a week, which is worse than the mistake almost every time.

The matrix should get shorter over time. One that keeps growing means I am still asking about things I should have learned.

Document 03

Inbox rules and response templates

Inbox management fails for one reason: the assistant answers things they should have escalated, or escalates things they should have answered. Both make you trust the inbox less.

Every email lands in one of six buckets

BucketWhat happens
AnswerI reply from a template, you never see it
ScheduleI book it inside your rules and confirm
ChaseI follow up, track it, close the loop
DraftI write it, you approve, I send
DecideBatched to you with a recommendation
ArchiveFiled, never surfaced

Nothing sits unsorted. An email that has not been categorised by end of day is the one that becomes a problem three weeks later.

Inbox zero, defined honestly

Zero does not mean everything is answered. It means every email has been categorised and has a next step. Some are waiting on you. Some are waiting on a third party. None are waiting on me.

Two of the templates

Chasing a contractor, second attempt

Hi [name], following up on the below. I have not heard back and [dependent thing] is waiting on it.

Can you give me a date by end of day today, even if the date is further out than we hoped?

The suspicious request

Not sent. Escalated. Any email asking for payment details, credentials, gift cards, or urgency plus secrecy goes to you unanswered, no matter who it appears to be from.

The chase log

Anything in Chase goes into a tracker with who, what, when asked, and when I follow up next. Default cadence: three days, then seven, then flagged to you. Nothing gets chased forever without you knowing it is stuck.

Document 04

Weekly check-in

Sent every Monday before your day starts. Written, not a meeting: a standing call costs you thirty minutes, this costs five and leaves a record.

  • Needs you this week. Three things at most, each with a recommendation and a deadline
  • Moving without you. What I am handling, listed so nothing is a surprise later
  • Waiting on other people. Who, what, since when, when I chase next
  • Your week at a glance. Meetings, deadlines, anything that eats a block
  • Closed since last week. The part that shows the cost of me is worth it

Rules I hold myself to

Three decisions maximum. If I bring you nine, I have not done my job. Every decision comes with a recommendation, never a menu. Deadlines on everything, because "when you get a chance" is how things rot. And nothing appears here for the first time if it was urgent: urgent things reached you when they happened, and knowing which is which is the job.

I send it even in quiet weeks. A weekly that says "nothing needs you, here is what moved" is the most reassuring message an assistant can send, and skipping it is how clients start wondering what you are doing.

Document 05

Meetings

Most meetings fail before they start, because nobody wrote down what it was for. Three templates used in sequence: an agenda the day before, decisions and actions captured during, a follow-up within two hours while people still remember agreeing to things.

The agenda includes a "not covering" line, naming the things people will otherwise raise. It saves more time than everything else combined.

An action item without an owner and a date is a wish. Every action goes into the chase log with a follow-up date, or the whole value of taking notes is destroyed.

Meetings I will push back on

Protecting your time means saying so when a meeting does not deserve it. I raise it once, suggest an alternative, then do whatever you decide.

SituationWhat I suggest instead
No decision neededA written update
One person talkingA recorded message
Same attendees, third time this monthOne standing slot, consolidated
Could be resolved in three emailsThree emails
No agenda by the day beforePostpone until there is one

Document 06

Monthly report

Two pages, within three working days of month end. It answers three questions: what happened, what it cost, and what I would change.

A retainer is easy to keep paying and easy to quietly resent. This is the document that makes the value visible, including when the honest answer is that a month was quiet.

  • What moved. Outcomes, not a task list
  • By the numbers. Emails handled, meetings scheduled, items chased to done, still waiting on others, hours used against the retainer
  • What is stuck. What is blocked, on whom, how long. The section most assistants leave out, and the one that earns trust
  • What I would change. One or two recommendations with reasoning
  • Coming up. Anything needing your attention early rather than late

Including hours used matters. If I am consistently under, I say so and suggest dropping a tier. Volunteering that is what makes the number believable when I say I am over.
